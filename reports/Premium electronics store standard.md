# Make Lahore Silicon a precise counter

Lahore Silicon should feel like a careful Hall Road sourcing desk that already knows the part, not like a flagship film and not like a million-row distributor. Premium, for this ten-part catalog of regulators, sensors, a Nano-class board, and a relay, is the feeling that a competent person packed the object and that the next number will not change. The ceiling is set by documented restraint — Apple’s material-first product site and progressive buy flow, Aesop’s “what the associate would say,” Framework’s one-accent configurator and honest stock states, Braun’s habit of naming each object by its job — and by Baymard’s 2024 finding that engineers leave when a datasheet, a matching drawing, or a stock count is missing. Specialist catalogs (DigiKey, Mouser, RS, Arrow, LCSC, Adafruit, SparkFun, Seeed) supply the facts to copy and the chrome to refuse. The Astro storefront already has the right counter: a content-collection catalog, a persistent cart, advance payment, and a shipping policy. It fails because the claims run ahead of the data, the browser sets the price, and the token file is not what the pages paint. Until the site is finished, an implementation agent should make every sentence provable from a field, keep both the non-technical buyer and the working engineer on one screen, and leave the final hues unlocked.

## Premium here means the part, the price, and the date agree

The buyer in the research is a maker, student, or technician. The emotion that survives that buyer is confidence: the shop is precise, the object is the one pictured, and checkout will not surprise them. Apple’s own AirPods Max award text, a Bronze Pencil in Personalized Digital at the 2021 One Show, says the site existed because many people could not try the headphones in person. The team used a scrollable high-fidelity video rather than a WebGL model “to achieve a richer, higher-quality rendering closer to the real product,” set studio photography beside it “so our audience could envision the scale and detail,” shifted from a white theme to a bold black theme in the sound section, and explained internals with a layered X-ray rather than a paragraph ([The One Club, AirPods Max product site](https://www.oneclub.org/awards/theoneshow/-award/38768/airpods-max-product-site/)). That is photography as evidence. It is also a flagship consumer object. Slow motion, a black hero, and an X-ray are a poor default for a handful of small parts. The decision that transfers is narrower: show the real surface, and do not decorate it.

Judd Smith, a designer on iPhone and iPad purchase flows, describes the later buy flow as progressive disclosure so that “product imagery, selection, decision support and product availability are contextualized by each step.” The numbers he publishes — **87% of shoppers need assistance**, **50% had fulfillment and availability top of mind**, and segment labels of Chooser/Loyalist **15%**, Research-driven **18%**, and Browsers **29%** — are his, from an undated portfolio page covering the iPhone 14 / iPad era, not an Apple press release ([Judd Smith, Apple Buyflow Evolution](https://www.juddzsmith.com/apple)). The pattern is still the one a small shop can use: stock, alternatives, and the next question appear as the person moves, in the same type as the product name. A third-party breakdown of the iPhone 16 Pro marketing page, Lab 47-46 on 20 February 2025, reads empty space as confidence and says luxury is what is removed, then argues that desire should precede specification. That reading is an analyst’s, not an Apple quote, and it is about a phone story. Applied to a known part number it conflicts with the electronics testing below ([Lab 47-46](https://lab47-46.com/iphone-16-pro-product-page-breakdown-part-2/)).

Aesop’s 2018 store case study is the service metaphor. Work & Co also says Fast Company named the site “Best Website Design”; the Fast Company article was not opened, so that title stays Work & Co’s claim ([Work & Co clients](https://work.co/clients/)). Framework’s agency writeup specifies the purchase experience as “a testament to the modularity” of the laptop: a configurator, price updating in real time, guidance that does not take control away ([Nebulab, Framework](https://nebulab.com/case-studies/framework)). Nirav Patel’s quoted feeling is ownership against disposability; Chris Lombardozzi says the store was “perfectly aligned with our mission.” The TIME and Red Dot citations on that page are product awards, not site awards. A third-party gallery updated 24 September 2026, not the design team, describes near-black and warm off-white, orange reserved for primary actions, Graphik plus DM Mono, and every product entry expanding to the same four links: product page, Specs, Shop parts, Downloads and guides. Stock is more than in or out: In stock, Coming soon, Out of stock, Pre-Order, and Register interest ([ecomm.design, Framework](https://ecomm.design/site/framework/)).

Teenage Engineering’s OP–XY page, as text extracted on 19 October 2026, talks about the object as a physical thing before it lists functions: all black, dimensions matched to the OP–1 field, grayscale graphics, bright red only for recording and parameter locks, then a specifications block including a **480 × 222 px** display ([teenage.engineering/products/op-xy](https://teenage.engineering/products/op-xy)). Braun Audio’s English homepage, also retrieved as text and not as a visual review of a product page, bag, or checkout, introduces speakers by job — “LE 01 - Stereo speaker for powerful hi-fi listening” — and then renames them “The masterpiece,” “The versatile design speaker,” “The smart minimalist” ([braun-audio.com](https://www.braun-audio.com/en-GLOBAL)). Dieter Rams’ “less, but better” was not verified as text on that site. Leica’s 2024 relaunch, as Jakala tells it, is an agency delivery story: an interactive scrollytelling page for the M11, plus reported engineering results of loading times reduced by a factor of three, a system “80% more stable,” and PIM-to-CMS sync “300% faster.” Those figures are the agency’s and were not independently audited here ([Jakala](https://www.jakala.com/en-us/insights/putting-an-icon-into-perspective)).

Baymard’s luxury article of 8 June 2021 puts Bang & Olufsen in a set with Chanel, Gucci, and the rest, and says a low product count and a high price make it reasonable to spend heavily on descriptions, photography, and cross-sells. The same article says these sites sometimes choose brand aesthetics over conversion because the job is often brand and the offline store. Copying their omissions copies a different business. The named electronics pitfall is Bang & Olufsen showing no arrival timing. Gucci’s “1–2 business days” still forced calendar math. Baymard’s recommendation is a date or a date range near the buy section. If a product is only labeled “out of stock,” the article says some shoppers look for alternatives and **“30% are likely to simply abandon.”** Footer links to shipping and returns are a basic expectation. In the accounts research cited there, **34% of users tried site search** to find non-product content such as a return policy, and none of the luxury sites in that benchmark supported it ([Baymard, 15 luxury pitfalls](https://baymard.com/blog/2021-luxury-ecommerce-benchmark)). A current Baymard luxury landing page lists eight brands and says the topic holds 73 product-page guidelines and 124 cart-and-checkout guidelines. The 2021 article discussed seven sites. Scores remain paywalled ([Baymard luxury research](https://baymard.com/research/luxury-goods)). Baymard’s Apple UX case study, reviewed most recently in January 2026, lists product page and spec sheet among reviewed templates. Performance scores are paywalled and were not read ([Baymard, Apple case study](https://baymard.com/ux-benchmark/case-studies/apple)).

Aesop’s Aromatique Candles microsite, a campaign rather than the shop, won an Awwwards Site of the Day with an overall **7.53/10**, design **7.87**, usability **6.57**, creativity **8.17**, content **7.83**, dev **6.98**, and accessibility **6.00**, on a two-color palette of `#000` and `#ffffff`, with horizontal layout, parallax, GSAP, and WebGL ([Awwwards, Aesop Aromatique Candles](https://www.awwwards.com/sites/aesop-aromatique-candles)). That is the trap in one scorecard: a voyage can win on design while use and access lag. A parts buyer reads omission as incompetence. The page that answers “is this the right part, in stock, at this price, by this date?” with atmosphere first forces a second click or a guess.

## Copy the facts, adapt the small table, refuse the chrome

Baymard’s 8 May 2024 study of B2B electronic components and machinery is the only substantial 2023–2026 public research on these distributors. It records **2,500+ hours**, **168 moderated sessions** with engineers, purchasers, and manufacturing professionals, **1,100+ medium-to-severe issues**, and **275+ guidelines**. The guidelines are paywalled. The public article covers product tables and product-page content. Distributor sites in the test set included Arrow, CoreStaff, DigiKey, Farnell/Newark/element14, Future Electronics, Mouser, RS, and TTI, inside a wider set of 15 manufacturer and distributor sites ([Baymard, B2B electronics](https://baymard.com/blog/b2b-electronic-components-machinery-launch)). A later public benchmark, 30 July 2024, scores five sites in this industry on 430+ parameters and 2,000+ scores. Individual scores are not public ([Baymard, 30 July 2024](https://baymard.com/research-articles/b2b-electronic-components-machinery-2024-benchmark)). DigiKey’s case-study page, reviewed July 2024, lists 432 elements and hides the scores ([Baymard DigiKey](https://baymard.com/ux-benchmark/case-studies/digikey)). RS’s case-study page says it was first benchmarked in July 2026, on 429 elements, scores hidden ([Baymard RS](https://baymard.com/ux-benchmark/case-studies/rs-online)). No Smashing Magazine or Awwwards teardown of DigiKey, Mouser, LCSC, Newark, Arrow, Adafruit, or SparkFun from 2023–2026 was found. Adafruit, SparkFun, and LCSC sit outside Baymard’s published distributor set. The well-known RS redesign writeup is from 23 November 2015 and was not fully fetched; it is a conversion case study, not a 2026 audit ([Econsultancy](https://econsultancy.com/the-ultimate-ecommerce-cro-ux-case-study-rs-components/)). Smashing’s search article is December 2009 ([Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2009/12/principles-of-effective-e-commerce-search/)).

What the 2024 sessions actually show is distrust of the photo and hunger for the deciding fact. A biomedical engineer at CoreStaff would not buy a force-sensing resistor with no datasheet. An electronics engineer at element14 did not trust a spring-loaded connector’s photo and opened the datasheet for a dimensioned drawing in millimetres and inches. A senior electrical engineer at ST’s store looked for the stock count first and said that 100 in stock versus 100,000 would change how many he bought. An electronics engineer at Arrow chose the semiconductor with **1,000 in stock** over a cheaper one with **30**. An RS participant had to add an out-of-stock logic gate to the cart to learn the replenishment date. A Mouser participant used a “View Dates” overlay and treated a date later this month or next month as acceptable. Two engineers tried to hand an Arrow cart to a colleague, found no share control, and one said he would not use Arrow for that reason. On desktop, a product table beat a card list because people scan attributes across rows. **One hundred percent of participants filtered or tried to filter.** They preferred filters above the table; a left sidebar stole columns. Sorting by price and by inventory was heavily used. Columns that mattered included part number, name, price including minimum order quantity, thumbnail, category-specific specs, lifecycle, inventory, and lead time. Attributes missing on some rows made comparison fail. On mobile, Baymard recommends a list, not a table. They tested desktop only, and report that these sites averaged **73% desktop traffic**, some as high as **83%**. That is traffic to those distributors, not a law for a hobbyist shop in Lahore ([Baymard, 8 May 2024](https://baymard.com/blog/b2b-electronic-components-machinery-launch); [Baymard product tables, 10 April 2024](https://baymard.com/research-articles/use-product-tables-for-desktop-product-listings)).

Live pages, observed as text on 19 October 2026 unless noted, show the same checklist. DigiKey’s US product HTML was blocked by a bot check, so DigiKey facts come from indexed product-page text and DigiKey’s own help posts, not from a rendered US layout, cart, or checkout. The indexed UK page for TI LM7805CT/NOPB shows manufacturer, manufacturer product number, a one-line description, a 12-week standard lead time, a datasheet, a two-column attribute table, **“In-Stock: 0”** plus **“45 expected in stock on 19-Oct-2026,”** unit price with and without VAT, and a statement that the image is a representation only. Those stock figures are a point in time, not standing inventory ([DigiKey UK, LM7805CT/NOPB](https://www.digikey.co.uk/en/products/detail/texas-instruments/LM7805CT-NOPB/3901929)). A tactile-switch page splits one manufacturer part into Tape & Reel, Cut Tape, and Digi-Reel, shows **28,147** in stock, separate ladders of quantity, unit price, and extended price, a **$7.00** Digi-Reel fee, and a note that tariff costs appear in the cart ([DigiKey, TL3330AF130QG](https://www.digikey.com/en/products/detail/e-switch/TL3330AF130QG/1426835)). DigiKey’s own part-search article uses a through-hole resistor category of more than **501,000** items and warns that filtering several parameters at once can return zero parts. The article does not give a publication date in the fetched text, so 501,000 is DigiKey’s example, not a 2026 census ([DigiKey part search](https://www.digikey.se/en/articles/how-to-use-digi-key-part-search)).

Mouser’s indexed TL52AF260Q page shows both part numbers, a datasheet, “Images are for reference only,” **“In Stock: 3,549,”** “Can Ship Immediately,” minimum 1, and the same three-column ladder ([Mouser, TL52AF260Q](https://www.mouser.com/ProductDetail/612-TL52AF260Q)). Its op-amp category table puts stock, expected dates, lead time, and break prices on the row itself ([Mouser UK](https://www.mouser.co.uk/c/semiconductors/amplifier-ics/)). LCSC prints minimum and multiple on the same line as the stock count, then the ladder, and “Every Order Guarantee 100% Authentic · 30-Day Return or Replacement.” Its ladders often start at the minimum, which is frequently not quantity 1 ([LCSC C347478](https://www.lcsc.com/product-detail/C347478.html)). RS regional pages for the same TI regulator show stock split by location, per-unit and per-pack prices, and a pack size that is not uniform: Norway prices a pack of 5, New Zealand a tube of 45 ([RS Norway](https://no.rs-online.com/web/p/voltage-regulators/7968060); [RS New Zealand](https://nz.rs-online.com/web/p/voltage-regulators/9222369)). Newark’s help center, not a fetched LM7805 product page, documents real-time stock, same-day shipment, backorder when quantity exceeds stock, and a long list of distinct states from “in stock” through “lead times not currently available” ([Newark, pricing and availability](https://www.newark.com/help-pricing-availability)). Arrow’s L7805CV narrative and spec list were fetched; the inventory widget was not in that fetch, so the regional “ships tomorrow” and “ships in 2 days” cards are from indexed text, not a confirmed live render ([Arrow, L7805CV](https://www.arrow.com/en/products/l7805cv/stmicroelectronics)). A no-stock Arrow page switches the button to Quote at a **4,000-piece** minimum ([Arrow, NCP81270MNTXG](https://www.arrow.com/en/products/ncp81270mntxg/on-semiconductor)).

Maker shops replace the parametric wall with sentences. Adafruit’s HC-SR04, product 3942, **$3.95**, marked In stock on the fetched page, states a narrower “best results” range of 10 cm to 250 cm against a headline range of 2 cm to 400 cm, warns that the Echo pin is 5 V logic, and includes two 10 kΩ resistors as a divider ([Adafruit, HC-SR04](https://www.adafruit.com/product/3942)). SparkFun’s HC-SR04, **$5.25**, puts Datasheet, Example Tutorial, and GitHub above the overview and names the four pins ([SparkFun, HC-SR04](https://www.sparkfun.com/products/15569)). Seeed’s old store URL for a bare HC-SR04 resolved, on fetch, to an unrelated breadboard. The maintained document is the Grove ultrasonic wiki, updated 7 January 2026, with voltage, a shared SIG pin, a version table, a schematic, and a do-not-hot-plug warning ([Seeed wiki](https://wiki.seeedstudio.com/Grove-Ultrasonic_Ranger/)). A forum reply quotes **USD 3.99** and a promotional page lists **$14.90**. Those prices conflict, and neither was confirmed on a fetched product URL ([Seeed forum](https://forum.seeedstudio.com/t/esp32-c6-not-working-with-grove-sensor/292606); [Seeed event page](https://www.seeedstudio.com/view/event/full_get.html)). Guest checkout was not walked on any of these sites.

For this shop the copy list is the buying facts: manufacturer and seller part numbers, a one-line job (“5 V, 1.5 A, TO-220”), a datasheet, HTML specs for the decisions the shop actually supports, a numeric stock count or a date when stock is zero, minimum and multiple, a three-column break table only if breaks exist, a photo of the stocked item or an explicit representative-image line, lifecycle or “this is the part we ship,” and pinout plus electrical limits in the first screen. NN/g’s 3 July 2016 article, older and still the public spec-page guidance, says put specs in HTML as well as PDF, use industry units, and name the standard. PDF alone is a poor online format, and technical buyers still expect the PDF ([NN/g, B2B specs](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/b2b-specs/)). The adapt list is the small-catalog version of the same idea. NN/g’s 9 February 2024 comparison-table article says a comparison table works for a small number of offerings when the content is consistent and scannable ([NN/g, comparison tables](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/comparison-tables/)). A 23 August 2024 update of an older Nielsen article says that when differences are only implied, people pick the wrong option, and that experts overestimate what is obvious ([NN/g, explicit differences](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/explicit-differences/)). Baymard’s 23 April 2024 article says a search box inside a filter should appear past about **15–20 options**, and that **46%** of test sites did not let users reliably search within filter types ([Baymard, 23 April 2024](https://baymard.com/blog/allow-search-for-long-filter-options-lists)). A catalog of dozens of parts does not need that machinery. It needs an exact hit on the manufacturer part number, the shop SKU, and a plain name, a short category list, and the few columns that actually differ. “Similar parts” should be a short human list, not an algorithmic substitute engine. Packaging should be one sentence (“loose TO-220 in an antistatic bag”), the way Seeed’s 2025 XIAO case and tape-and-reel posts treat packaging as care, not three orderable SKUs ([Seeed packaging, 12 May 2025](https://www.seeedstudio.com/blog/2025/05/12/seeed-studio-xiao-gets-a-packaging-upgrade-durable-reusable-and-tiny/)).

The refuse list is the chrome that exists to manage hundreds of thousands of SKUs, contract pricing, and multi-warehouse allocation. Refuse parametric filters for attributes that do not vary here, search-inside-filter, separate part numbers per tape style, reel fees, regional price stacks, contract-price icons, quote-only as the default for a part on the shelf, a replenishment date that appears only after add-to-cart, representative photos with no disclaimer, lifestyle copy that omits voltage and logic level, apparel-style merging of distinct part numbers, a 200-line BOM tool, ECAD for every module, manufacturer lead times the shop cannot honor, and tariff or ECCN blocks unless the shop actually exports that way. Baymard’s general product-list guidance to combine variations into one list item is illustrated with apparel and is a weak fit for an LM7805 and an AMS1117, which are different parts ([Baymard, updated 9 September 2025](https://baymard.com/research-articles/current-state-product-list-and-filtering)). No published study was found that tests a catalog under about 100 SKUs against a DigiKey-style interface, so the refuse list is an inference from the large-catalog findings plus the live pages, not a direct experiment. Conversion effects of a single price versus a ladder, for a premium component shop, were not found in 2023–2026 sources.

From the premium brands, copy the restraint and the service, not the campaign. Copy Framework’s rule that one accent is for the action, that the price moves because the configuration changed, and that a performance claim carries its test method. Copy Aesop’s associate: the page should know what a careful salesperson would say, and a returning customer should restock without a brand essay. Copy Braun’s job sentence. Copy Apple’s progressive disclosure and the idea that the picture is evidence of material, edge, and scale. Copy a footnote under any number, in Framework’s pattern. Adapt the Apple sequence — feeling, then proof, then specification — into one specific sentence above a spec block that is already on the first screen. A flagship module can have one well-lit picture. The rest of the catalog should scan. Refuse the cinematic marketing URL split from a separate shop URL. Bonobo Creative’s independent audit of Teenage Engineering, not a commissioned rationale, found the product story and the purchase on different pages; the proposed fix was one page ([Bonobo Creative](https://bonobocreative.com/tengine)). Refuse Teenage Engineering’s retrieved voice (“wet fantasies,” “it’s sexy”) even while keeping the physical precision. Refuse SSENSE-style editorial as a shopping model. A 17 July 2023 Medium case study by nchmim, who states they are not associated with SSENSE, reports a **23-user** survey in which **“83.45%”** were dissatisfied with search. Treat that percentage as the author’s small survey, not an industry measurement ([Medium, nchmim](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/ssense-re-design-f3aacb65ada1)). Refuse award-site craft: horizontal voyages, WebGL, parallax, and a usability score that lags the design score. Refuse luxury’s omitted delivery date. Two further Baymard index summaries, “Avoid Quick Views for Spec-Driven Product Types” and a note on horizontal tabs hiding core content, were seen only as index text. The articles were not opened, so those percentages are not used here.

## Commerce rules stay binding only with their printed caveats

The public 2025–2026 Baymard benchmarks say most large retail sites are still mediocre, and they say why people leave. They do not say what a ten-SKU Lahore shop will gain. The product-page benchmark updated 18 March 2026 covers **155+** US and European sites and **30,000+** ratings. **52% of desktop, 62% of mobile, and 64% of app** sites are “mediocre or worse.” Only **48% of desktop and 38% of mobile** are “decent” or better. A few desktop sites and one mobile site are “good.” None are “perfect” or “state of the art.” Baymard’s interpretation is that users more often leave because medium issues accumulate ([Product Page UX 2026](https://baymard.com/research-articles/current-state-ecommerce-product-page-ux)). The checkout benchmark updated 25 November 2025 rests on **41,000+** scores: **64% of desktop and 63% of mobile** sites are mediocre or worse, only **2%** are good, and **15% of desktop** sites are poor or very poor. The article says the average large site “can gain as much as a **35%** increase in conversion rate” from checkout design. The abandonment statistics page, updated 22 September 2025, states the modeled figure as **35.26%**, then multiplies it by **“$738 billion”** of US and EU sales to get **“$260 billion”** recoverable. The page does not show the arithmetic inputs, the year of the base, or an independent A/B test. **Do not treat 35.26% as the expected lift for this shop** ([Checkout UX 2025](https://baymard.com/research-articles/current-state-of-checkout-ux); [Cart abandonment statistics](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate)).

The same statistics page’s average cart-abandonment rate is **70.22%**, the mean of 50 studies from 2006 through a 2025 figure, and the checkout article rounds it to 70%. The average mixes window-shopping with genuine failure. Among people who were not “just browsing,” the page updated 22 September 2025 prints **40% extra costs too high**, **20% delivery too slow**, **19% did not trust the site with the credit card**, **18% the site wanted an account**, **17% checkout too long or complicated**, **17% the website had errors or crashed**, **13% returns policy unsatisfactory**, **12% could not see or calculate the total up front**, **10% the card was declined**, **9% not enough payment methods**, and **7% don’t know**. A checkout-article survey of 1,026 US adults also says **18%** abandoned because of an account, which matches. A February 2024 wave reported by eMarketer (n=1,012) had extra costs at **48%**. A Statista chart dated 28 May 2025, citing Baymard, shows a different 2025 distribution (39% extra costs, 21% slow delivery, and so on). That chart was not opened beyond the search extract, and it does not match the Baymard page fetched on 19 October 2026. The numbers used here are the ones printed on baymard.com.

The product-page article’s public pitfalls, with the site-failure rates it prints, have to be filtered for parts. **57%** do not use size buttons instead of a closed dropdown; that is an apparel finding. **37%** do not offer “in scale” images, and **42% of users** try to judge size from pictures and, without a reference, some abandoned rather than read the written dimensions. That transfers: a dimensioned drawing, a grid, or a familiar object, not a human model. The human-model pitfall (**23%** don’t) is limited by the article to products that are worn. **89%** do not make save easy without an account; in a study of 1,193 people, **21%** said they rely on save features. **81%** do not show price per unit when the same product is sold in different amounts. **67%** do not show a total-order-cost estimate near the buy section. **44%** do not link or summarize the return policy in the main product content. In the Consideration study of 1,032 respondents, **60%** look for the return policy on the product page. The article also says **15%** of 1,026 US shoppers abandoned because the return policy was unsatisfactory. The statistics page now says **13%** for the same reason. Both numbers are printed. They are not reconciled. Gifting (**78%** don’t) is low relevance. Staff replies to negative reviews (**89%** don’t) were read in testing as evidence of reachable support; no conversion percentage is published. A carousel of reviewer photos (**63%** don’t) was used as a check on whether site photos were representative. Do not invent ratings while the catalog has no real orders.

Shipping-cost behavior is older than the implementation rate. The 20 June 2017 study says **64%** of test users looked for shipping cost on the product page before add-to-cart, **43%** of sites showed none there, **16%** did not show it even in the cart, and **21%** of US shoppers in that era abandoned because they could not see the total before checkout. Shipping mattered more when it was a large share of a cheap item. Best-to-worst estimate methods in that test were a flat lowest rate, an IP geo estimate, the signed-in user’s last destination, and a stated range, each labeled with its assumption ([Show shipping costs on product pages](https://baymard.com/research-articles/show-shipping-costs-on-product-pages)). The 64% figure is not a 2026 retest. The later, different statistic is the March 2026 rate: 67% still do not put a total-cost estimate near Buy. A 27 June 2023 article, not electronics-specific, says **41%** of sites gave a speed such as “2 business days” rather than a delivery date, and that participants calculated arrival themselves ([Baymard, 27 June 2023](https://baymard.com/research-articles/shipping-speed-vs-delivery-date)). The November 2025 checkout article says **48%** do not use a delivery date or range, and **83%** do not express an order-by cutoff as a countdown in the user’s terms. Static “1pm ET” was misread as the delivery date. NN/g’s taxes, fees, and shipping article — opened via a search snippet, publication date not captured — says shoppers “strongly disliked unexpected cost increases,” and that surprise fees at the end were often enough to make them leave and to make them think the site was trying to trick them ([NN/g, taxes, fees, and shipping](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ecommerce-taxes-fees/)).

Checkout design findings that still bind this shop, from the November 2025 article: guest checkout is not the most prominent option on **62%** of sites. An earlier article of 17 January 2023 said **47%** of sites that offer guest checkout do not make it prominent; the 2025 article is the later benchmark. A guest option as a text link, or below the sign-in fields, was missed, and on mobile an autofocused email field can cover the guest button with the keyboard. **65%** do not avoid overly complex password rules. Baymard says strict rules plus a fragile reset can cause up to a **19%** checkout-abandonment rate among existing account holders, and recommends a 6–8 character minimum only. That is a usability recommendation, not a security standard. **97%** do not implement cart quantity as buttons, or as buttons plus a field that replaces the current digit rather than appending to it. **61%** do not mark both required and optional fields. When only optional fields were marked, **32%** of participants skipped at least one required field. “All fields are required” at the top was missed. **49%** do not explain why a phone number is required. **“Over 70%”** of survey respondents were reluctant to give a phone number; the sample size for that item is not printed. **94%** do not use error text that names the specific failed rule. An ideal flow can be **12–14 form elements** (7–8 if only fields count). The average US checkout in their benchmark shows **23.48 elements** by default (**14.88 fields**). A line about 60 leading sites and 39 potential improvements sits next to older research wording and may not be the 2025 score set. The checkout guide of 23 April 2026 says the best checkouts feel linear, quotes Jamie Holst that a non-linear checkout “confuses and intimidates customers,” and names concrete patterns: a progress indicator that maps 1:1 to the steps, buttons labeled “Continue to Payment,” no mid-flow cross-sells or newsletter pop-ups or forced accounts, guest checkout as the default, browser back that does not wipe the form, and a review step that does not introduce new fees ([Baymard, checkout UX guide](https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-flow-ux-optimization)).

NN/g’s product-page guideline of 24 November 2019, older and still the public must-have list, says one image is rarely enough, descriptions should not be “wordy or fluffy,” the same facts should appear for every product in a category, and for data-heavy specs “comparison tables may be the best way.” Must-haves are a descriptive name, a recognizable image with an enlarged view, price including product-specific charges, options, availability, a clear add-to-cart with clear feedback, and a concise description. 360° photos, AR, and customization are “fancy,” worthwhile only when flawless. In their mattress example, Casper showed free shipping on the product page and Tuft & Needle revealed it only in the cart ([NN/g, ecommerce product pages](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ecommerce-product-pages/)). The mobile-checkout article of 2 December 2018 says open the cart from the cart icon, give each line a Remove control, do not require a separate Update, put guest checkout above the fold, put the order summary at the top on mobile, make autofill work because a broken autofill is blamed on the site, and offer one familiar wallet such as PayPal or Apple Pay rather than a flood of options. Wallet pay was perceived as more trustworthy than typing a card. No abandonment percentage is attached ([NN/g, mobile checkout](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-checkout-ux/)). The full NN/g search report (139 guidelines) and trust report (53 recommendations) are paid and are not used as numeric evidence. A search snippet’s “27% of task failures” for ecommerce search was not opened and is not used.

Lists and search are broad-retail benchmarks, useful as floors and dangerous as apparel templates. Updated 9 September 2025, **58% of desktop and 78% of mobile** sites are mediocre or worse on product lists (**21,000+** scores, **170+** sites). **80%** do not offer three or more thumbnails in the list. Two additional thumbnails are called a minimum for visually complex goods; a component is not evaluated that way. **14%** do not let users combine several values of one filter. **51%** lack at least one of price, ratings, color, size, and brand. **20%** do not show applied filters. **69%** lack at least one common sort; the body also says **68% of desktop and 69% of mobile**, and both rates are printed. Horizontal filter bars were only comfortable with a small set, about eight on apparel sites. Search, updated 29 April 2026, covers **10,000+** ratings: **56%** fail to support search adequately, and **46% of desktop, 58% of mobile, and 64% of apps** are mediocre or worse. Exact title, model, or SKU search still has issues on **12%** of sites, while the same section also says a third of sites tested were mediocre or worse on exact search. Those two rates are both printed and are not the same claim. If an exact model search misses, testers concluded the site did not carry the item. Feature queries fail on **39%** of sites. Product-type queries have issues on **20%** ([Ecommerce Search UX 2026](https://baymard.com/research-articles/ecommerce-search-query-types)). Autocomplete design, published 2 August 2022 and not refreshed in the 2026 article, was present on **80%** of sites and done right on **19%** ([Autocomplete design](https://baymard.com/research-articles/autocomplete-design)). The only fully public spec-sheet article is 27 March 2018, so its rates are not a 2026 measurement: **50%** of spec sheets were hard to scan, sheets longer than about **20 specs** need titled groups, **20%** used multiple columns which some users misread as a comparison of two products, and eye-tracking showed alternating row color made label-to-value tracing faster ([Spec sheet scannability](https://baymard.com/research-articles/spec-sheet-scannability)).

A BigCommerce survey of May 2023, n=1,006 B2B buyers, is a platform vendor’s survey, not a lab study. The top stated frustrations with percentages in the prose were inaccurate pricing and shipping costs **40%**, slow load **29%**, and poor customer support **28%**. **31%** said technical issues at checkout were what would most likely stop them. The checkout feature they valued most was a display of the complete, accurate total. Cards were the most-used payment method. The report’s “use a one-page checkout” sentence is staff marketing. An agency blog repeating the same parts-page pattern cites no study and is not evidence. Guideline #417, updated July 2026, states that unavailable rows add clutter; the advice is redacted ([Guideline 417](https://baymard.com/guidelines/417-product-status-filters)). No public percentage was found for how many sites show a numeric stock count. No verified rate was found for repeat purchase, for the share of buyers who require a datasheet, or for guest versus account among technical buyers. The rules that follow from this, for this catalog, are these. On every product page, next to the price, show the lowest shipping cost for one unit, or the free-over threshold, with the assumption in the label. If the rate depends on destination, show a range or a postal-code field on the page. In the cart and on the shipping step, show a date or a date range. If the shop cannot promise a carrier date, say the dispatch window in calendar terms and do not imply a promise. Guest checkout is a primary button, labeled as such, and account creation comes after the order. The form shows only what is needed to ship and pay, marks every field required or optional, hides company and address line 2 behind a link, and asks for a phone number only with a one-line reason or leaves it optional. Quantity is minus, a field that replaces the current number, and plus. Remove is a separate control. Errors name the broken rule, sit on the field, keep the typed value, and do not appear before the person leaves the field. Specs are one column, grouped, with alternating rows. A sheet longer than about twenty rows leads with the specs that choose the part and repeats them in the full table. Stock is a number, or an honest lead time, and add-to-cart will not allow more than that number. The datasheet is on the first screen, and the voltage is also in HTML. Search returns the product for the exact manufacturer part number or the shop SKU. A miss on an exact part number means the shop does not sell it. Distinct orderable parts stay distinct. List rows show part number, price, a truthful thumbnail, and the one or two specs that distinguish siblings. Ratings are not required while there are no real reviews; if reviews exist later, answer the negative ones in a visually distinct staff style. The cart can be resumed, and a line list can be copied as plain text. Contract pricing, net-30, punchout, and a multi-person approval workflow are not required by any finding that this shop must build them. Offer a card and one wallet the shop can actually clear. Do not print payment logos for methods that are not offered. Compatibility and “replaces” appear only when the shop knows them. NN/g’s 10 June 2018 expectations article is qualitative: participants became skeptical when inventory, fees, dates, or product facts were vague or wrong ([NN/g, expectations](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ecommerce-expectations/)). A fabricated “100% genuine” line is weaker than a datasheet and a part number that match the marking.

Accessibility has no public manual audit that says premium electronics shops fail WCAG 2.2 at a given rate. What was measured is automated detection. The WebAIM Million, a February 2025 crawl of the top 1,000,000 homepages, found **50,960,288** errors, an average of **51** per page, down **10.3%** from 56.8 in 2024. **94.8%** of homepages had a detected failure, versus 95.9% in 2024 and 97.8% six years earlier. Automated tools miss many failures, so full conformance is rarer than “no detected errors.” The six types that are **96%** of detected errors: low-contrast text on **79.1%** of homepages, missing alternative text on **55.5%** (18.5% of images lacked alt; **44%** of those were linked images), missing form labels on **48.2%** (34.2% of inputs unlabeled), empty links **45.4%**, empty buttons **29.6%**, missing document language **15.8%**. Skipped heading levels appeared on **39%** of pages. A main landmark was on **42.6%**, a search landmark on **16.6%**, a skip link on **15.3%**, and one in ten of those skip links was broken. Pages using ARIA averaged **57** errors versus **27** without it. That is not a finding that ARIA itself causes inaccessibility. Shopping homepages, labeled by an AI classifier rather than a hand taxonomy, averaged **71.2** detected errors, **39.8%** above the 51-error average. That is homepages, not product or checkout templates, and not a hand-check of premium brands ([WebAIM Million 2025](https://webaim.org/projects/million/2025/)). The HTTP Archive Web Almanac 2025 accessibility chapter says only about **30%** of sites had sufficient contrast in a figure description, and the prose says **31%** of mobile sites meet minimum contrast. The page disagrees with itself. Passing contrast is still a minority ([Web Almanac 2025 accessibility](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/accessibility)).

WCAG 2.2, a W3C Recommendation of 5 October 2023, adds criteria that this shop’s sticky header, drawer, steppers, and checkout will hit ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/); [What’s New in WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/new-in-22/)). **2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (AA)** says author content must not completely hide a focused control. Partial overlap still passes AA. **2.5.8 Target Size (AA)** asks for at least **24 by 24 CSS pixels**, or spacing so that 24px circles do not overlap. The older 44 by 44 target is AAA. Meeting 24px is the AA bar, not a claim that 24px is comfortable. **3.3.7 Redundant Entry (A)** says information already entered in the same process is available to select. **3.3.8 Accessible Authentication (AA)** says a password step must allow a password manager and paste, or offer another method. **3.2.6 Consistent Help (A)** says a help mechanism that repeats across pages stays in the same relative order. **2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide (A)** requires a pause control for motion that starts automatically, lasts more than five seconds, and sits beside other content. **1.4.3** requires **4.5:1** for normal text and **3:1** for large text. **1.4.11** requires **3:1** for UI boundaries that carry meaning. **1.4.1** says color is not the only means of conveying information. **4.1.3** requires a status message for add-to-cart that does not yank focus. Checkout inputs need visible labels, not placeholders acting as labels, autocomplete tokens, and errors that are text tied to the field. The drawer must move focus inside, trap Tab, and return focus on Escape. Contact stays in the same footer position on the catalog, the cart, and checkout.

Performance budgets that current Google guidance states as pass/fail are the Core Web Vitals at the **75th percentile** of real visits, mobile and desktop separately: **LCP within 2.5 seconds**, **INP within 200 milliseconds**, **CLS of 0.1 or less**. Poor lines are LCP over 4 seconds, INP over 500 milliseconds, and CLS over 0.25. A page passes only if all three are good. The CLS good line of 0.1 was chosen because internal testing found shifts of 0.15 and above consistently disruptive; the cited Chrome UX Report slice is from April 2020. The threshold page does not publish a 2026 revision. INP replaced FID. There is no published universal image-kilobyte budget ([Web Vitals](https://web.dev/articles/vitals); [threshold methodology](https://web.dev/articles/defining-core-web-vitals-thresholds); [INP](https://web.dev/articles/inp)). On more than **70%** of pages the LCP element involves an image. Guidance is to give that image explicit dimensions, a modern format, a srcset matched to the slot, `fetchpriority="high"`, and not `loading="lazy"`. AVIF “tests have shown greater than **50%** savings when compared to JPEG in some cases,” which is not a kilobyte cap ([Image performance](https://web.dev/learn/performance/image-performance); [Key performance issues for images](https://web.dev/learn/images/performance-issues)). A Google Flights Fetch Priority experiment moved LCP from **2.6 s to 1.9 s** on one page. It is not a revenue test ([Fetch Priority](https://web.dev/articles/fetch-priority)). A web.dev blog on LCP misconceptions was not opened in full, so no further breakdown of request delay versus bytes is claimed. A statement that **40%** of Chrome UX Report sites miss good LCP was undated in the extract.

Documented business effects are large-site experiments and correlations, not a forecast for a parts catalog. Vodafone, 17 March 2021, A/B tested a landing page at about 100,000 clicks and 34,000 visits per day per version. Field LCP was **31%** better. Results were **8% more sales**, **15%** higher lead-to-visit, and **11%** higher cart-to-visit. DOMContentLoaded got **15% worse**, which is why they warn against optimizing the wrong metric ([Vodafone](https://web.dev/case-studies/vodafone)). Rakuten 24 reports a field correlation of a good LCP with up to **61.13%** higher conversion, and a separate one-month A/B test on one landing page with revenue per visitor **+53.37%** and conversion **+33.13%**. Those percentages are specific to that page ([Rakuten 24](https://web.dev/case-studies/rakuten)). Renault’s correlation across more than 10 million visits says a 1-second LCP improvement can track with a **14 percentage-point** lower bounce rate and a **13%** increase in conversions. It is not a controlled A/B test, and the outcome is lead forms ([Renault](https://web.dev/case-studies/renault)). redBus is cited on a web.dev page as a **7%** sales increase after an INP improvement. The case-study URL returned **404** on 19 October 2026, so **7% is unverified** ([Why speed matters](https://web.dev/learn/performance/why-speed-matters)). No opened 2025 or 2026 case study measures conversion change for a small catalog or for electronic parts. Do not quote Rakuten’s 33% or Vodafone’s 8% as the expected return. The justified claim is that, on those measured sites, faster LCP and stabler layout moved sales, and that a crash or a long checkout is already a Baymard abandonment reason.

No opened Baymard, NN/g, or web.dev page finds that countdown scarcity, a false “only 2 left,” trust-seal clip art, or a newsletter modal increases trust. Baymard’s cutoff countdown is a true order deadline. Fake stock would contradict both the component study and NN/g’s accuracy finding. Live chat is not a research requirement. The closest public test that a visible human response increased confidence is the review-reply finding, which has no conversion percentage. No opened study isolates the conversion effect of a trust seal, a physical address, or a chat widget for a small shop.

## Lock type, space, and motion; leave the hues open

The craft notes refuse a finished palette on purpose. What they lock is structure. Apple’s Human Interface Guidelines, read on 19 October 2026 from a mirror because the official typography URL returned only a title, say to minimize the number of typefaces, to publish named text styles that bundle weight, size, and leading, and to avoid Ultralight, Thin, and Light at interface sizes. Optical sizing is the font’s job when a variable font has an opsz axis. San Francisco’s tracking table is for San Francisco, in thousandths of an em, and is not a table to paste onto another family. The mirrored changelog lists updates through 16 December 2025. An iOS-specific tracking table was not in the mirrored macOS and tvOS tables ([Typography, Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography); [WWDC20](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2020/10175/)). Vercel’s Geist typography is consumed as classes that set size, line-height, letter-spacing, and weight together. Roles are named, not generated from a ratio. `text-copy-14` is “the most commonly used text style.” Mono is paired a step smaller than the text it sits beside. Faces are Geist Sans and Geist Mono ([Geist Typography](https://vercel.com/geist/typography)). Stripe Apps do not allow arbitrary font faces. Text style is a named font token ([Stripe Apps style](https://docs.stripe.com/stripe-apps/style)). The fetched Stripe page showed `font: 'heading'` and then omitted the rest of the style list, so “subheading, body, caption” is not confirmed from this pass. No official public Linear type specification was found. Karri Saarinen’s 28 March 2024 note talks about reducing noise and raising density. It does not publish a scale ([Linear redesign](https://linear.app/now/how-we-redesigned-the-linear-ui)). Third-party scrapes of linear.app are observations of a marketing page, not a spec to copy.

Matthew Butterick’s rules for body text are point size, line spacing, line length, and font. Web body size **15–25 pixels**. Line spacing **120–145%** of the size. Average line length **45–90 characters**. He says to avoid “goofy fonts, monospaced fonts, most free fonts, and system fonts—especially Times New Roman and Arial” for reading text, and to add **5–12%** letterspacing to all caps or small caps, not ordinarily to lowercase ([Butterick, key rules](https://practicaltypography.com/summary-of-key-rules.html); [Letterspacing](https://practicaltypography.com/letterspacing.html)). OpenType `tnum` and CSS `font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums` put figures on a uniform width. Tabular width is often the default and “cannot be safely assumed.” `slashed-zero` exists for the O-versus-0 distinction ([Microsoft OpenType features](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/opentype/otspec140/features_pt); [MDN, font-variant-numeric](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/font-variant-numeric)). Google Fonts Knowledge says tabular lining figures are for tables and for numbers that change while staying aligned, and that proportional oldstyle figures sit better in lowercase prose. A catalog’s digits are data. Oldstyle figures are a poor default ([Google Fonts, understanding numerals](https://fonts.google.com/knowledge/introducing_type/understanding_numerals)). No 2023–2026 primary source in this set prescribes a single mathematical ratio such as 1.25 for product UI. No foundry specimen was chosen. Pairing advice is structural and still has to be checked in the chosen fonts at 13–16px and at display size.

The type rules for this shop, marked as opinion in the notes and adopted here because they follow those sources, are: one text family chosen on purpose, plus one monospace used only for part numbers, values, and short technical strings. Product descriptions stay in the text face. If the text face has an opsz axis, set `font-optical-sizing: auto`. All-caps labels get Butterick’s 0.05–0.12em. Body and table prose get none. Prices, stock counts, and columns of part numbers get tabular lining figures. Part numbers that mix letters and digits also get a slashed zero when the font has it. A part number inside a sentence can stay proportional so it does not look like a ripped-out cell. Named roles — display, title, heading, body, label, caption, mono — each lock size, line-height, weight, and tracking together. Reading passages follow Butterick’s measure. Spec cells do not. Two weights for UI text, plus italic, are enough. Light and thin weights fail Apple’s legibility rule at catalog sizes. Inter, or the raw system stack, as the only typographic choice because it was the default, is a Butterick failure whether or not a font-usage census says it is common. That census was not run. The current storefront loads Inter and JetBrains Mono. That is a fact about the code, recorded below. It is not a decision that Inter is the finished face.

Spacing follows a 4px base. Material’s spacing-methods page aligns components to an **8dp** grid and type to a **4dp** baseline, and allows type to leave that grid when it is optically centered in a button or a list row. Touch targets of at least 48×48dp are Material’s older metric, not the WCAG AA floor ([Material spacing methods](https://m2.material.io/design/layout/spacing-methods.html); [Android grids and units](https://developer.android.com/design/ui/mobile/guides/layout-and-content/grids-and-units)). Material 3’s grids pages returned only titles in fetch, so the 4/8 rule above is from Material 2 and current Android docs. Stripe’s confirmed spacing tokens are **0, 2, 4, 8, 16, 24, 32, 48** pixels, with 2px as the published exception under the 4px step ([Stripe Apps style](https://docs.stripe.com/stripe-apps/style)). The shop scale inferred from those two systems is **0, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64**. The 2px step is only for hairline offsets, focus rings, and icon optical alignment. A raw 13px or 18px margin in a component is a defect. Catalog rows share one alignment: part numbers left, prices right or decimal-aligned, in tabular figures. Centered cards fight that job. The grid breaks on purpose in four cases only: optical centering inside a control, a 1px keyline, a pixel of icon shift inside its box, and a full-bleed photo whose crop is the object. A decorative column grid is not the catalog. Geist’s Grid component is for marketing pages where the guide lines are the design, and the docs say it is overkill for ordinary cards ([Geist Grid](https://vercel.com/geist/grid)). One reading measure, inside Butterick’s 45–90 characters, and a wider width for the table. When text is resized to 200% (WCAG 1.4.4), stack the price under the name. Do not shrink the type to save the row. No official Apple web container width, and no official Linear or Vercel 4/8/16 spacing-token table, was retrieved. A single correct pixel max-width for a parts table was not published.

Catalog motion is a short response to an action. Apple’s WWDC18 “Designing Fluid Interfaces” says an interface feels fluid when it is an extension of the person: instant response, interruption at any moment, spatial consistency, and springs that start at **100% damping** so a tool does not overshoot. “A spring doesn’t need to overshoot.” Bounce is for a gesture that already has momentum, not for a tap. Tools “require us to not be in the way with introducing unnecessary motion” ([WWDC18](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2018/803/)). Mapping 100% damping onto a CSS cubic-bezier is an approximation. The honest web equivalent is ease-out with no bounce. web.dev says ease-out durations of about **200–500ms** are the UI default, and bounce and elastic eases of **800–1200ms** do not belong on a product that is not trying to be playful ([Choosing the right easing](https://web.dev/articles/choosing-the-right-easing)). Material 3 has moved to springs and says the older duration system is unmaintained, though the published pairs remain: standard **300ms** on screen, exits often **200ms**, and extra-long **700–1000ms** for ambient motion that does not involve input ([Material easing and duration](https://m3.material.io/styles/motion/easing-and-duration)). Older Material guidance says desktop transitions of about **150–200ms** because they are seen often ([Material duration and easing](https://m1.material.io/motion/duration-easing.html)). The spring parameter table that replaces those tokens was not retrieved.

The catalog — home, category, product, cart, checkout — gets hover and press as an opacity or background change around **100–150ms**, and a drawer or dialog around **200–250ms**, ease-out or a spring with no bounce, interruptible. Add-to-cart is a short confirmation, not a thumbnail flying across the page. Quantity digits do not animate through intermediate values. Do not animate `width`, `height`, `top`, or `margin` on catalog rows. Under `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce`, drop transforms and keep a short opacity change so the person can still tell that a panel opened. Do not hide that behind a custom toggle that defaults to on. Apple’s reduced-motion criteria say to detect the system setting and to replace meaningful motion rather than delete it ([Apple reduced motion](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/manage-app-accessibility/reduced-motion-evaluation-criteria/); [prefers-reduced-motion](https://web.dev/articles/prefers-reduced-motion)). Marketing motion, if any, is one entrance on the home introduction. No marquee, no gradient drift, no parallax, no autoplaying carousel. Vercel Ship 2025’s ferrofluid and scroll-linked zoom are a conference site, not a catalog ([Vercel Ship](https://vercel.com/blog/designing-and-building-the-vercel-ship-conference-platform)). No source in this set measured catalog-task time against animation length. The split is an inference from Apple’s tool guidance and from Geist’s separation of marketing grids and plain lists. Linear has not published a motion spec here. Third-party millisecond notes are not a Linear standard.

Color is roles, not a brand lock. WCAG 2.2 requires **4.5:1** for normal text, **3:1** for large text and for meaningful UI boundaries, and forbids color as the only signal. AAA is **7:1** for normal text. MDN recommends aiming at AAA and at least meeting AA. Large text is at least 18pt, or 14pt bold. There is no AA exception for “de-emphasized but still informative” spec labels. If muted text is normal-size text, it is 4.5:1. Inactive text is the incidental exception, and a price or a part number is never inactive text ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/); [MDN contrast](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Accessibility/Guides/Understanding_WCAG/Perceivable/Color_contrast)). Geist’s public system maps scale steps to jobs — default background, hover, border, secondary text, primary text — and says to draw each border once, because overlapping translucent borders get louder, and to keep shadows translucent ([Geist Colors](https://vercel.com/geist/colors)). The fetched page did not include literal hex or P3 values. Do not copy them. Material 3’s `ColorScheme` is a role set: surface, onSurface, onSurfaceVariant, outline, primary and onPrimary, error and onError, in light and dark. `surfaceTint` is an elevation overlay a catalog can refuse. Use spacing and one line token to separate rows ([ColorScheme](https://developer.android.com/reference/kotlin/androidx/compose/material3/ColorScheme)). Material’s design-token overview did not return its article body; the three-layer model (reference, system, component) is from the search snippet of that URL ([Material design tokens](https://m3.material.io/foundations/design-tokens)). Stripe’s fetched page confirmed the property model and the example values `container`, `neutral`, `primary`, and `success`, not the full enum.

The token set, with no hues chosen, is: `surface`, `surface-raised`, and a rare `surface-sunken`; `ink` and `ink-muted`, each at least 4.5:1 on the surfaces they sit on, and `ink-faint` only for inactive text; `line` at 3:1 when the line is how a control is seen, and `line-subtle` only for decorative rules inside a table that already aligns; one accent for the primary action and the selected row, with `on-accent` at 4.5:1 on the fill, and not a second text color for headings; a separate status set — success, warning, danger, info — each with an on-color and a muted container, plus a word or an icon. Check pairs, not swatches. Light and dark, if both exist, are two assignments of the same roles. Do not invert the accent into a neon because dark mode is on. A specific shop palette was intentionally not chosen. The six looks the research brief asked to refuse are not catalogued by Apple, Linear, Stripe, Vercel, Material, or WCAG as “AI defaults.” They conflict with the rules above, and no 2023–2026 study in this set measured how often they appear. Refuse them anyway: cream paper with a clay accent that fails the contrast pair, a near-black page whose only decision is an acid accent, a broadsheet of drop caps and hairlines with no editorial reason, an unchosen Inter or system stack, an identical card grid for every SKU, and gradient-mesh heroes. The signature that fits the research is structural: the text face, part numbers in the mono with tabular lining figures and a slashed zero, prices aligned. Radius stays a token, 0 or 4px for rows and inputs. Shadows are for overlays that float, and they are translucent.

Architecture follows the same three layers. Reference tokens are not used in components. System tokens are the roles. A page touches variants, not raw CSS: a button is `primary`, `quiet`, or `danger`, and there is no className escape hatch for color or spacing. A part is data — part number, manufacturer, short name, price, currency, stock state, package, and spec rows of label, value, and unit — not a blob of HTML. The product page, the table, and the cart read the same fields. Astro pages stay thin. Interactive islands are the cart, the quantity control, and the mobile nav. Tokens live in one CSS file. If a value is used twice, it is a token. If it is used once and it is not on the scale, it is wrong. No Astro-specific design-token case study from these companies was found. The content shape is an inference. None of the cited design systems define a product-spec schema. Vercel’s note that generated UI feels specific only when tokens constrain it is the operating warning for every later implementation pass ([AI-powered prototyping with design systems](https://vercel.com/blog/ai-powered-prototyping-with-design-systems)).

## Conclusion

The non-technical buyer and the working engineer are not two audiences who need two sites. They are two reading orders on one screen, and the research already names both: Braun’s job sentence and Aesop’s associate for the person who needs to be told what the object is for, and Baymard’s stock integer, datasheet, and scannable row for the person who arrived with a part number. Lahore Silicon feels premium when those occupy the same quiet block and when every marketing sentence is still true after the server prices the order. The award sites and the distributor sites are ceilings for different mistakes. Copying Opal or the Aesop candles voyage buys a design score and a usability debt. Copying DigiKey’s filters buys clutter this catalog cannot fill. The work that remains is almost entirely the removal of claims the ten JSON files cannot support, and the addition of the few fields a Hall Road desk would already have written on the bag.
